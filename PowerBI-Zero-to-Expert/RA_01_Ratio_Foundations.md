# ⚖️ RA1 · Ratio Foundations: The Grammar of Shared Numbers

> "In the ratio 4:3" is four words that decide how ₹1,75,000 of profit becomes ₹1,00,000 and ₹75,000 — no calculator argued, no feelings consulted. A **ratio** is a comparison of two same-kind quantities written a:b; a **proportion** says two ratios match. Every partnership deed, every recipe, every map scale, every "divide this fairly" runs on this grammar. Today: simplify like a machine, split money by equal-parts logic, chain ratios through common terms, and catch the ONE exam trap this module breeds — confusing a ratio's TERMS with a ratio's WHOLE. ShopKart's store network is our playground.

---

## 🎯 Objectives

- State that ratios compare **like-for-like** quantities and are unit-free.
- **Simplify** ratios via HCF and convert fraction↔ratio fluently.
- Execute **equal-parts division**: splitting totals in a:b (the k-parts method).
- **Chain** A:B with B:C into A:B:C by making the middle common.
- Apply **duplicate/sub-duplicate** scaling (sides ↔ areas ↔ volumes).
- Know why ratios aren't additive: a:b + c to both terms → a NEW ratio (moving toward 1).

## 📘 Concepts

### 1.1 What a ratio IS (and isn't)

```
A:B = 3:4  <--->  A/B = 3/4    (term-to-term swap, both legal)
Simplify like fractions by HCF:  84:144  ->  divide by 12  ->  7:12
Ratios need SAME units & same kind: Rs to Rs, kg to kg.
A ratio has NO unit of its own. Never "3:4 rupees".
```

**The TERM vs WHOLE law (the module's only trap, pre-disarmed):** a split in 2:5 cuts the whole into 2+5 = 7 parts. The first share is **2/7 ≈ 28.6%**, NOT 40%! Terms tell the FRACTION OF THE WHOLE only through their sum. (PC1's base law nods; it's the same reflex in ratio clothes.)

### 1.2 Equal-parts division (the k-parts engine)

**ShopKart profit share:** ₹1,75,000 between Rakesh and Arjun in 4:3.

```
Parts = 4 + 3 = 7   ->   one part = 1,75,000/7 = 25,000
Rakesh = 4 x 25,000 = 1,00,000     Arjun = 3 x 25,000 = 75,000
CHECK: 1,00,000 + 75,000 = 1,75,000 ✔  (ALWAYS re-add; splits must tile)
```

Three-way splits work identically: parts a+b+c, tile-check at the end. This single pattern solves profit shares, rent allocations, recipe scaling, and shop-floor wage pools.

### 1.3 Chaining ratios (common middle)

Given A:B = 3:4 and B:C = 5:6, find A:B:C. B shows two faces (4 and 5) — normalize B to the LCM(4,5) = 20:

```
A:B  = 3:4  = 15:20
B:C  = 5:6  = 20:24
A:B:C = 15:20:24 ✔
```

Another: A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 4:5 → A:B = 8:12, B:C = 12:15 → **8:12:15** ✔. The middle term is the translator between two ratio dialects.

### 1.4 Scaling laws: duplicate & sub-duplicate

Power play: if sides scale a:b, AREAS scale a²:b² ("duplicate ratio") and volumes a³:b³. Two photo posters with sides 2:3 → areas 4:9 → paper cost 4:9 (useful when costing banners!). Reverse: areas 9:16 → sides **3:4** ("sub-duplicate"). Maps, photos, packaging — all run this law.

### 1.5 Ratios are NOT additive

Add the same number to both terms of 3:5 (+4 each) → 7:9 — a DIFFERENT ratio; the fraction moved 3/8 = 37.5% → 7/16 = 43.75%, i.e., **toward 1 (100%)**. Memorize this as the ABCD law: Adding Both terms Changes the Direction toward 1. Ages-riddle gold: father:son 35:20 becomes 40:25 = 8:5 in five years — same family, new ratio. Only multiplication/division preserves a ratio; addition never does.

## ❓ Quiz

**Q1.** ₹1,75,000 split 4:3 pays out:
- (a) ₹87,500 each
- (b) **₹1,00,000 and ₹75,000 — 7 equal parts of ₹25,000, taken 4 and 3; the re-add check (1,00,000 + 75,000 = 1,75,000) must always tile**
- (c) ₹1,05,000 and ₹70,000
- (d) ₹1,25,000 and ₹50,000

**Q2.** A:B = 3:4 and B:C = 5:6 give A:B:C equal to:
- (a) 3:4:6
- (b) **15:20:24 — B speaks with two faces (4 and 5), so normalize to LCM 20: 3:4 = 15:20 and 5:6 = 20:24, then read the trio**
- (c) 6:8:6
- (d) 3:5:6

**Q3.** 84:144 in simplest form:
- (a) 8:14
- (b) **7:12 — divide both terms by HCF 12; simplification is legal ONLY because multiplicative operations preserve ratios — addition would not**
- (c) 6:8
- (d) 4:7

### ✅ Answers

1. **(b)** — equal-parts with a compulsory tile-check; splits must rebuild the total.
2. **(b)** — the common-middle LCM move; two-step chains are the exam's favorite cousin.
3. **(b)** — HCF-divide, always; and remember the ABCD law when someone "adjusts both sides".
