# 💰 PL1 · Profit & Loss Foundations: CP, SP & the Base Law

> Every kirana uncle in Delhi runs this module in his head: buy at one price, sell at another, pocket the difference. The exam version adds exactly one twist that wrecks half the hall — **profit% and loss% always ride on the COST PRICE.** Not the selling price, not the MRP, not vibes. One base, burned into your reflexes today, plus the multiplier engine (CP × 1.20 = SP) that makes every P&L question a one-liner. ShopKart's own ₹71 → ₹100 basmati bag is our opening case study, and yes, we'll expose the margin-vs-markup dialect war that confuses even MBA interviews.

---

## 🎯 Objectives

- Define **CP, SP, Profit, Loss** and compute P/L in rupees instantly.
- Enforce the **Base Law**: P% = P/CP × 100, L% = L/CP × 100 — CP is the only base.
- Drive the **multiplier engine**: SP = CP × (1 ± rate), both directions.
- Reverse cleanly: CP = SP ÷ (1 + p) — the ÷-multiplier from PC2, now with a shop counter.
- Speak both dialects: exam **profit% (on CP)** vs business **margin% (on SP)** — and convert (29% margin ↔ 40.8% markup).

---

## 📘 Concepts

### 1.1 The three prices and the one formula

```
CP (cost price)   = what the seller PAID
SP (selling price)= what the buyer PAID
Profit  = SP - CP   (when SP > CP)   Loss = CP - SP (when CP > SP)
```

**ShopKart basmati:** procured at ₹71, shelf price ₹100 → Profit = ₹29 per bag. Karol Bagh moves ~54 lakh-rupees of quarterly revenue on exactly this arithmetic, scaled.

### 1.2 THE BASE LAW (tattoo this reflex)

```
P% = Profit / CP x 100        L% = Loss / CP x 100
```

The basmati bag: P% = 29/71 = **40.8%** (exam law). PC1's Law of the Base in trader clothes: percentages are meaningless without their base, and in P&L the base is ALWAYS CP unless the question explicitly says otherwise.

### 1.3 The multiplier engine (forward & reverse)

```
SP = CP x (1 + profit-rate)     CP 250, +20%  -> SP = 250 x 1.20 = Rs 300
SP = CP x (1 - loss-rate)       CP 500, -12%  -> SP = 500 x 0.88 = Rs 440
REVERSE: CP = SP / multiplier   SP 1,150 at +15% -> CP = 1,150 / 1.15 = Rs 1,000
```

The multiplier IS the profit percent wearing a calculator. Multiplying builds selling prices; dividing recovers cost prices. (Your Percentages course called this the division-multiplier; Finance called it discounting's cousin. One engine, many counters.)

### 1.4 The dialect war: profit% (CP) vs margin% (SP)

Same bag, two boardrooms:

```
EXAM dialect:   profit% = 29/71  = 40.8%   (rides CP)  "markup"
BUSINESS dialect: margin = 29/100 = 29.0%  (rides SP)  ShopKart's P&L speaks this
CONVERT: margin = markup / (1 + markup)   ->  0.408/1.408 = 0.29
         markup = margin / (1 - margin)   ->  0.29/0.71   = 0.408
```

ShopKart's official **gross margin is 29%** (₹29 gross on every ₹100 of sales — COGS eats 71%). An exam asking "profit%" on the same bag wants 40.8%. Same transaction, two dialects — the analyst who knows BOTH never gets caught translating in a meeting.

### 1.5 Breakeven and the zero-line

SP = CP → no profit, no loss → **breakeven**. Anything below CP bleeds. Simple, but it's the floor of every pricing decision, and PL7's capstone builds a whole defense on it: ShopKart's ₹71 COGS is the concrete floor under the ₹100 shelf tag — discounts may approach it, never cross it (without a written reason).

## 🧪 LAB — Foundation reps (10 min)

Base stated BEFORE every percent. Multipliers written as × or ÷.

1. Tracking desk: bought a mixer at ₹1,800, sold ₹2,160. P and P%?
2. A kurta sold ₹1,150 at 15% profit. CP?
3. Sold a chair ₹440 at 12% loss. CP? (Careful — the multiplier is below 1.)
4. Dialect drill: markup 25% → margin in business-speak? Margin 20% → markup?
5. ShopKart mini-case: festive lights bought ₹240/set, shelved ₹300. Exam-profit% and P&L-margin — both.

**Solutions (hide till done):** 1) P = ₹360; 360/1,800 = **20%** · 2) 1,150 ÷ 1.15 = **₹1,000** · 3) 440 ÷ 0.88 = **₹500** (loss-sales ride the 0.88) · 4) markup 25% → margin 25/125 = **20%**; margin 20% → markup 20/80 = **25%** — the dialects dance in symmetric pairs · 5) P% = 60/240 = **25%** (exam); margin = 60/300 = **20%** (business).

## 💪 Exercises

1. Fresh Farms paneer lot: CP ₹5,400, sold to a hotel at ₹6,210. P%?
2. A phone sold ₹27,600 at 15% profit. CP? (Reverse-multiply, no guessing.)
3. Sold a cycle ₹3,680 at an 8% loss. CP?
4. Convert: markup 60% → margin? Then margin 37.5% → markup?
5. One-liner for the interview: why do exams and CFOs both claim to compute "profit%" yet disagree on the number?

### ✅ Selected answers

1. 810/5,400 = **15%**.
2. 27,600 ÷ 1.15 = **₹24,000**.
3. 3,680 ÷ 0.92 = **₹4,000** — note how round the reverse always lands; exam-setters pick clean CPs, so a messy division is your error-alarm.
4. 60/160 = **37.5%** margin; 37.5/62.5 = **60%** markup (the mirror dance).
5. Exams ride CP by law (markup), CFOs ride SP by convention (margin) — same rupees, different denominators. Quote the base BEFORE the number and the confusion dies at birth.

## ❓ Quiz

**Q1.** The basmati bag: ₹71 cost, ₹100 shelf. Exam-law profit% is:
- (a) 29%
- (b) **40.8% — 29/71: profit% rides the COST price by law; the 29% figure is business margin on SP — same bag, two dialects, and PL7 prices wars with this distinction**
- (c) 25%
- (d) 20%

**Q2.** CP ₹250, target profit 20%. The selling price:
- (a) ₹270
- (b) **₹300 — 250 × 1.20; the multiplier IS the profit percent wearing a calculator, and forward questions are one multiplication deep**
- (c) ₹280
- (d) ₹310

**Q3.** A kurta SOLD at ₹1,150 earned 15% profit. Its CP:
- (a) ₹1,050
- (b) **₹1,000 — reverse the multiplier: 1,150 ÷ 1.15; dividing is CP-recovery, and a clean landing (round number) confirms the arithmetic**
- (c) ₹977.50
- (d) ₹1,225

### ✅ Answers

1. **(b)** — base first, always. The point of the whole module in one bag of rice.
2. **(b)** — forward = build with (1 + rate). No formula sheet needed.
3. **(b)** — reverse = divide by the multiplier. Clean CPs are the exam's gift to division.
