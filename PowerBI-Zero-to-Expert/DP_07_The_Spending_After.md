# 🎯 DP7 · The Spending After — Repairs, Replacements, Overhauls

> The machine is capitalized, the clock is running — and now the spending starts. Every rupee spent ON an existing asset faces the same two-gate recognition lock from DP1: probable future benefits, cost measurable reliably, PLUS being a genuine renewal rather than the maintenance of the status quo. Spend that merely RESTORES or services dies in P&L; spend that REPLACES a component or RENEWS an even-inspection cycle earns a place in the carrying amount — and evicts the old slice in the same breath. Master this module and you can read any capex storm: which crores are genuinely new muscle and which are last year's opex standing on tiptoes trying to reach the balance sheet (the WorldCom move, DP11's marquee crime).

## 📘 Concepts

### 7.1 Same lock, second door — subsequent costs face para 7 too

Costs of day-to-day servicing — labour, consumables, small parts — are NOT recognized in the carrying amount: they are profit-and-loss when incurred (para 12). The standard is blunt about why: those costs restore or maintain the benefits already measured; they add no NEW future benefit. Everything else — parts replaced, inspections completed, upgrades bolted on — runs through para 7's lock just like the original purchase: probable benefits + reliable cost → into the carrying amount it goes. The by-line that ends bar-room debates: servicing pays for the PAST's consumption; capitalization buys the FUTURE's.

### 7.2 The furnace lining canon — replace one, bury one (python-verified)

Your induction furnace needs its refractory lining replaced every few years. The old lining's cost was never separately recorded — composite-era books. New lining costs **₹6L** today. The para 13/para 70 two-step:

1. **Capitalize ₹6L** — replacement of a component meets the recognition lock.
2. **Derecognize the old part's carrying amount.** Not recorded? The standard hands you the estimate ladder: use the cost of the NEW part as the indicator of what the OLD part cost when installed — say ₹4L equivalent — and net off the consumption already suffered: if its life was 60% consumed, the old carrying amount is 4 × 40% = **₹1.6L** — derecognized as a loss in P&L.

Net P&L first-year effect: a ₹1.6L derecognition loss plus the new lining's own depreciation — versus expensing ₹6L instantly (wrong: understates assets, drags the year's profit harder and differently-timed) versus the truly criminal option, capitalizing the new and FORGETTING the old (a zombie ₹1.6L of dead lining haunting the depreciation schedule forever — auditors love finding that one).

### 7.3 Overhauls ride as components — the DP3 handoff continues

DP3's rule stands: a major inspection or overhaul cost is recognized in the carrying amount as a REPLACEMENT when criteria met — ₹4L every 4 years = a component depreciated ₹1L/yr, each new overhaul renewing the slice and derecognizing the old sliver. Ship dry-dockings, airline D-checks, kiln re-linings: all the same animal. What NEVER rides along: the forced standstill losses during overhaul (period cost), spares below component significance (inventory-expense), and any betterment EXTRA scope (which is its own separate capitalization decision, not part of the overhaul slice).

### 7.4 The five enhancement tests — capital or cosmetic?

When a spend sits between "repair" and "upgrade," audit-grade judgment runs these probes (document all five in the file):

1. **Restoration vs enhancement** — returns capacity to the original standard (expense) versus pushes it beyond (candidate capital).
2. **Life extension** — merely preserves the scheduled life (expense) versus MATERIALLY extends it with evidence (capital, triggering a DP4-style review of useful life).
3. **Capacity or quality uplift** — output volume, product quality, or spec level genuinely improved (capital) versus same output (expense).
4. **Operating-cost reduction** — the spend structurally lowers future cost per unit (capital candidate) versus merely prevents cost rise (expense).
5. **Component identity** — does it map to a recognizable replaceable part of the DP3 register (capital with derecognition) versus a blur of servicing activity (expense)?

Fails all five? Expense with clear conscience. Passes one firmly with evidence? Capitalize — and test what old slice dies in the same journal.

### 7.5 The fraud axis — opex on tiptoes

Here is why this module is the radar's home ground: capitalization lifts today's profit (cost dodges P&L) at the price of quietly heavier depreciation for years — a bargain a cornered management takes every time. The historical monument is WorldCom's line-cost fraud: billions of operating expenses re-labelled capital expenditures. The analytical tells (DP11 does the full autopsy): capex jumping while revenue and capacity growth sag; "intangible nature" classifications multiplying; peers' capex-to-sales ratios drifting lower in the same industry weather. The discipline that protects you: every capitalized rupee must name its future benefit and its dying ancestor — no orphan assets, no zombie parts.

## 🧪 LAB — Capex Committee of One (10 min)

The plant file of Aravali Castings lists post-acquisition spends on Press No.7 (carrying ₹40L, DP3-register components identified). Route each: **CAPITALIZE** (and name the derecognition sidekick) or **EXPENSE** — one ruleline each.

1. Annual preventive maintenance contract ₹1.5L.
2. Replacement hydraulic cylinder: new ₹6L; old cylinder estimated ₹4L original cost, 60% consumed.
3. Safety-guard retrofit mandated by new factory rules ₹2L (no output change, no life change — a new attached device).
4. Control-system upgrade raising throughput 12% ₹9L.
5. Repainting and pit-filling of the press body ₹0.8L.
6. Five-year statutory overhaul ₹5L (overhaul slice exists in the register, fully depreciated).

**Why this matters:** this exact schedule, with your initials on each route, is what a statutory audit working paper looks like.

**🔑 Lab answers:**
1. **EXPENSE** — day-to-day servicing, para 12; maintaining benefits is the price of owning, not a new benefit. 2. **CAPITALIZE ₹6L; derecognize ₹1.6L** (4 × 40% remaining) — replacement two-step, estimate ladder via new-cost indicator. 3. **EXPENSE** (or optional capital of a trivial accessory — but canon-strict: expense) — restores compliance status; changes neither future benefit stream, capacity, nor life; fails all five tests. 4. **CAPITALIZE ₹9L** — measurable capacity uplift (test 3); review useful life and the controls component it attaches to. 5. **EXPENSE** — cosmetic restoration of, at best, the shell's condition; no future benefit. 6. **CAPITALIZE ₹5L** — major inspection renewal; old slice fully depreciated, so derecognition is ₹0 — clean swap, new 5-year clock at ₹1L/yr.

## 💪 Exercises

1. Finance argues the ₹6L cylinder should be expensed "because last year we expensed a ₹6L gearbox repair." Two-sentence steering correction (hint: consistency is not precedent when the facts differ).
2. A senior manager proposes capitalizing the ₹1.5L maintenance contract "because it extends useful life." Cross-examine with test 2.
3. Write the derecognition journal logic (no journal entries, T-account English) for the cylinder swap.
4. Why does the estimate ladder lean on the NEW part's cost — what assumption does that make, and when does it break?

### ✅ Selected answers

1. Consistency governs like-for-like facts, not labels: a gearbox REPAIR that restored function was correctly expensed; a cylinder REPLACEMENT that retires a component and re-arms a new one meets the recognition lock. Same price, different substance — the register, not the invoice template, decides.
2. An AMC PRESERVES the scheduled life — it does not extend it beyond plan; test 2 demands material extension WITH evidence. Future-benefit gate fails; para 12 wins; expense it.
3. New cylinder ₹6L into PPE (cylinder component); remove old cylinder's estimated carrying ₹1.6L from the register; recognize ₹1.6L loss in P&L ("derecognition loss"); cash/payable foot the ₹6L. Register now shows a fresh ₹6L component with its own clock — no zombie.
4. It assumes parity of specification and price level between old and new — approximation, defensible when the design is stable; it breaks when today's cylinder is a different generation (pricier for features), and then the estimate must be normalized (strip the upgrade delta) before recomputing the old carrying amount.

## ❓ Quiz

**Q1.** Day-to-day servicing of an existing machine:
(a) capitalizes if the contract is annual
(b) is expensed as incurred (para 12) — it restores or maintains benefits already counted; the two-gate recognition lock (probable new benefits, reliable cost) is what any spend must pass to join the carrying amount
(c) capitalizes if above ₹1L
(d) is deferred to a maintenance reserve

**Q2.** The cylinder canon: new ₹6L, old estimated ₹4L cost 60% consumed:
(a) capitalize ₹6L and keep the old slice inside the register too
(b) capitalize ₹6L AND derecognize the old part's ₹1.6L carrying amount as a P&L loss — using the new part's cost as the indicator of the old when history is silent, so no zombie component keeps depreciating after death
(c) expense ₹6L and keep the old slice
(d) capitalize the ₹1.6L difference only

**Q3.** Which spend capitalizes?
(a) repainting and pit-filling the press body
(b) a control-system upgrade demonstrably raising throughput 12% — measurable future-benefit enhancement (test 3 passes), versus servicing, compliance cosmetics, and repainting, which all restore the status quo and die in P&L
(c) the annual preventive-maintenance contract
(d) safety-guard retrofit with no output or life impact

### ✅ Answers

1. **(b)** — servicing is the rent of ownership, never a new asset.
2. **(b)** — replace-and-bury: ₹6L in, ₹1.6L out, estimate ladder for silent histories.
3. **(b)** — only the throughput upgrade buys measurable future benefit.
