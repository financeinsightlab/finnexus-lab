# 🎯 WM5 · Insurance & Protection — The Floor Under the Compounding

> Insurance is the one product in finance where the best outcome is that you "wasted" the premium — like a fire extinguisher, its success is its silence. But India's insurance habit inverts this: endowment plans, ULIPs, and money-back schemes mixing 6% "returns" with 4× cover. This module enforces the **Separation Law**: insure separately, invest separately, and check claims history before premiums.

---

## 🎯 Objectives

- Enforce the Separation Law: insurance ≠ investment, ever (the Double Plan and ULIP autopsies)
- Size term cover: 10-15× annual income + liabilities − assets
- Build the health stack: base floater + super top-up + the waiting-period minefield
- Read insurer quality: claim settlement ratio, solvency, persistency
- Cover the forgotten risks: personal accident, disability, and the parents' dilemma

## 📘 Concepts

### 5.1 The Separation Law — autopsy of a confused product

TV7's client pitched "₹5L/yr for 8y, money doubles in 12y" — Rule-of-72 translation: **6% contractual**, taxable, 12-year lock, thin cover. The ULIP variant (WM3's Verma case): ~3% all-in cost gap ≈ **48% of the 30-year corpus** gone (factors 3,495 vs 1,829). Insurance companies earn their margins from *confusion between protection and returns*. The Law's two sentences: **(1) Protection is bought from the cheapest, highest-quality term insurer. (2) Wealth is built in instruments you own separately — never as a rider, bonus, or "maturity value" of a policy.** Any document that says both "cover" and "returns" on page 1 is the enemy of at least one of those words.

### 5.2 Term cover sizing — the 12× anchor with arithmetic

**Cover = 10-15× annual income + outstanding liabilities − liquid assets** (the income-replacement logic: 25× expense for full FI mirrors WM1). Sharma-style: income ₹18L/yr (₹1.5L/mo), home loan ₹26.6L, assets ₹42L, kid aged 3 → human-life cover ≈ 12 × 18 = 216L + 26.6 − 42 ≈ **₹2Cr** — split two term policies of ₹1Cr each (diversifies insurer risk and nomination flexibility). Price of sanity: **₹1Cr term at age 30 ≈ ₹800-₹1,100/mo** for a healthy non-smoker — less than a streaming subscription bundle, covering a family's entire future. Buying term at 30 vs 40: premiums roughly **double** (₹1k → ₹2k/mo class) — delay is the most expensive product feature in the industry.

### 5.3 The health stack — floors, not fantasies

Hospital Inflation runs ~14%/yr in India (medical CPI >> headline 6%) — so ₹5L cover bought today is worth ~₹1.5L-equivalent in 8 years. The stack: **(1) base floater ₹10L** (family of 3, ~₹18-25k/yr at 30s ages); **(2) super top-up ₹25-90L** with ₹10L deductible (~₹6-12k/yr — catastrophic cover is cheap because it triggers rarely); together ~₹1Cr effective for ~₹30k/yr. Minefield reading: waiting periods (PED 2-4y — disclose EVERYTHING on the proposal form; undisclosed thyroids are how claims die), co-pay clauses, room-rent sub-limits (proportionate deduction — the fine print that halves claims), restoration benefits. **Parents:** senior-citizen premiums hurt (₹40-60k+/yr for ₹10L at 60s) — sequence them onto super top-ups + accept co-pay — but *some* cover beats the family-GoFundMe every single time.

### 5.4 Insurer quality — three numbers before any premium

1. **Claim Settlement Ratio** (CSR): % of claims paid out — term insurers ≥ 98% class leaders (IRDAI publishes annually); below ~90% = walk away regardless of price.
2. **Amount Settlement Ratio**: % of *claim value* paid — catches insurers approving small claims while disputing big ones; ≥ 90% healthy.
3. **Solvency ratio**: statutory minimum 1.5; ≥ 1.8 comfortable. Add persistency (61st month ≥ 60% = customers stay — service proxy). The premium is a 30-year contract against the company's promises: **the cheapest term quote with a 94% CSR is more expensive than the 99% one at +15% premium.** Buy the payer, not the price.

### 5.5 The forgotten shelves — accident, disability, critical illness

Life cover pays only on death; a spinal injury paying ₹0 income × 40 years is the un-modeled scenario: **Personal Accident cover** (₹1Cr ≈ ₹5-8k/yr — absurdly cheap because morbidity ≠ mortality probabilities) + **Critical Illness rider/standalone** (₹10-20L lump on diagnosis — buys treatment choices and 18 months of no-panic). Term FMCG-law: **insure the income stream, not the sentiment** — homemakers insure too (their replacement cost: childcare + logistics ≈ ₹40-60k/mo). Review the whole stack every 2 years or at any "life event" (marriage, child, loan, job switch): the cover that fit the bachelor is a tissue over the family man.

## 🧪 LAB — Rebuild the Mehta Protection Stack (10 min)

**Setup:** Rahul Mehta 36, Sneha 34; income ₹32L + ₹8L; 2 kids (7, 3); parents dependent (61, 64 — no cover); home loan ₹55L outstanding; assets ₹85L (SIP 55 + EPF 20 + FD 10); current policies: endowment plan ₹1L/yr premium (SA ₹20L — "maturity in 2035, bonus projected!"), ULIP ₹60k/yr (3y paid), employer health cover ₹5L (job-dependent!), no term, no accident cover.

**Do this:**
1. Size Rahul's correct term cover with the 12× anchor (and Sneha's — decide if she needs cover and why).
2. Score the endowment plan: translate to its real return (₹1L/yr × 20y → ~₹28L maturity class) and its cover adequacy vs the 12× need. Verdict + how to exit (paid-up vs surrender).
3. Rebuild health: employer ₹5L is job-locked — design the stack with amounts, premium budget, and the two parents' approach.
4. Add the forgotten covers: personal accident + CI with logic lines.
5. Write the family's Separation-Law refactor as 5 instructions the Mehtas execute this quarter.

**Why this matters:** The Mehtas are the archetype client: asset-rich-ish, protection-broken, and paying ₹1.6L/yr in premiums for ₹20L of cover — exactly upside-down. Rebuilding them is the WM craft in miniature: every rupee re-routed from confused products to clean ones buys both more cover AND more corpus, simultaneously, which is why insurance audits feel like finding money.

**🔑 Lab answers:**
1. Rahul: 12 × 32 = ₹3.84Cr + 55 (loan) − 85 (assets) ≈ **₹3.5-4Cr term** (say two policies ₹2Cr + ₹1.5Cr across different insurers). Sneha: 12 × 8 = ₹96L + half the loan-share ≈ **₹1-1.5Cr** — yes, she needs cover: her income AND her unpaid household logistics both need replacing (WM5's FMCG-law covers homemaker economics even at ₹0 formal income).
2. Endowment: ₹1L × 20 → ₹28L maturity ⇒ IRR ≈ **~3.3%** (vs EPF 8.25 / index ~12) and ₹20L of cover vs ₹3.5Cr needed — fails BOTH sides of the Separation Law. Exit: **make paid-up** (stop premiums, keep reduced benefit to maturity — surrender recovers ~30-60% of paid value and crystallizes the loss; paid-up usually leads). Redirect the ₹1L/yr to term premiums (~₹40k covers the whole family stack) + the ₹60k to SIP.
3. Health stack: **own base floater ₹10L** (~₹25k/yr, job-independent — the employer ₹5L vanishes on resignation and it's time-boxed until 60) + **super top-up ₹90L @ ₹10L deductible** (~₹12k/yr) = ₹1Cr effective for ≈ ₹37k/yr. Parents: base ₹5L each + shared ₹25L super top-up with 10-20% co-pay accepted (~₹80k-1L/yr total for both — the premium shock is the *bill arriving in installments*; plan for it in the budget).
4. PA ₹1Cr for Rahul + ₹50L Sneha (≈ ₹8k+4k/yr — disability income protection); CI standalone ₹20L each (~₹15k/yr at 36/34 — buys treatment-choice and 18-month recovery runway; claims are binary, less fightable than health indemnity claims).
5. Q-execution list: (1) Apply term ₹3.5Cr (Rahul) + ₹1.5Cr (Sneha) — medicals scheduled; (2) Endowment → paid-up notification; ULIP surrender at 5y-lock expiry (₹60k/yr → index SIP); (3) Buy ₹10L own floater + ₹90L super top-up + parents' stack; (4) Add PA ₹1Cr/₹50L + CI ₹20L/₹20L; (5) Total premium ≈ ₹1.7-2L/yr (barely above the old ₹1.6L) — but now buying **₹3.5Cr+ life cover and ₹1Cr health** instead of a 3.3% savings account in a tuxedo. Update nominations everywhere (WM6 preview).

## 💪 Exercises

1. **Sizing.** Karthik: income ₹24L/yr, loan ₹30L, assets ₹25L, single, parents 55/58 (dependent). Term cover size; name the nominee question that follows.
2. **ULIP autopsy.** "Pay ₹50k/yr for 10y, cover ₹5L, projected fund ₹9L at 8%" — write the Separation-Law reading in two sentences with the counter-product.
3. **Claim forensics.** Insurer X: CSR 99.1%; Insurer Y: 94.2%, premiums 18% cheaper for same ₹2Cr cover. Which and why — one paragraph a non-finance spouse understands.
4. **Health minefield.** Patient hid a thyroid condition (2 years pre-policy) at proposal; claim for unrelated cardiac ₹6L arrives. What happens and what's the design lesson?
5. **Employer cover trap.** Mehta argues "my company gives ₹5L free — why buy my own ₹10L?" Three crisp counters.

### ✅ Selected answers

1. Anchor: 12 × 24 = ₹2.88Cr + 30 − 25 ≈ **₹2.9-3Cr** — but note the dependency question: nominations track the *obligation map*, and being single with dependent parents 55/58 means the cover exists mainly to age-proof THEM until his own savings can; revisit the whole split at marriage.
2. *"₹5L of cover for ₹50k/yr is a coin purse pretending to be a parachute — the same ₹50k buys ₹2Cr of term (₹15k) with ₹35k/monthly SIP left over, and the SIP's 12% beats the plan's ~5.5% IRR for 10 years straight."* The counter-product is never another insurance product; it's a term plan plus a fund you own outright.
3. X, and tell her this: *"We pay ₹1Cr-scale premiums for exactly one event — the claim. A 94.2% CSR means roughly 1 in 17 families fights at the worst moment of their lives; 99.1% means 1 in 110. The 18% 'saving' is a discount coupon for a courtroom. We buy the payer, not the price."*
4. The insurer can repudiate for **non-disclosure of a material fact** — even an unrelated claim can be contested within the early years (post-2024 rules limit repudiation after 3-8 years for some non-disclosures, but fraud stays contestable). Design lesson: **disclose everything on the proposal form** — the premium rise for a declared thyroid (~₹1-3k/yr) is infinitely cheaper than a repudiated ₹6L cardiac bill.
5. (1) **It ends with the job** — resignation/layoff/retirement leave a 55-year-old shopping for fresh cover with 25 years of medical declarations priced in (morbidity loads, rejections); (2) ₹5L is 1980s medicine — one cardiac stay at a metro private hospital can invoice ₹8-12L (14% medical inflation); (3) **super top-ups cost ~₹7-12k/yr** — the employer plan is the *dessert*, carry your own restaurant. Own the base, always.

## ❓ Quiz

**Q1.** The Mehtas' endowment (₹1L/yr → ₹20L cover, ~3.3% IRR projected) should be judged as:
(a) A disciplined savings plan — keep it
(b) A double failure: negative-real returns AND cover at ~1% of his need — make paid-up and redirect premiums to term + SIP
(c) Better than term because "money comes back"
(d) Fine if the bonus projection holds

**Q2.** Correct term sizing for a ₹18L/yr earner with ₹26.6L loan and ₹42L assets is closest to:
(a) ₹20L —whatever the agent suggests
(b) ₹2Cr — 12 × income + liabilities − assets, split across two insurers
(c) ₹50L — round numbers feel right
(d) No cover needed since assets exist

**Q3.** Choosing between CSR 99.1% (₹1,150/mo) and CSR 94.2% (₹950/mo) for identical ₹2Cr term:
(a) The 94.2% one — term is a commodity, buy cheap
(b) The 99.1% one — the product you are actually buying is the *claim payment itself*; the ₹200 premium gap is a courtroom-discount coupon
(c) Flip a coin
(d) Buy both ₹1Cr each

### ✅ Answers

1. **(b)** — 3.3% IRR against 6% inflation is purchasing-power erosion with a life-insurance sticker, and ₹20L covers ~6 months of his family's needs. The ₹1.6L/yr premium buys the entire correct stack (term ₹3.5Cr + health ₹1Cr + PA/CI) with change — the audit math feels like finding money because it IS.
2. **(b)** — 12 × 18 + 26.6 − 42 ≈ ₹2Cr: income replacement is the point (the corpus must fund 25× family expense if he vanishes). Splitting across insurers diversifies claim-handling risk and lets nominations track obligations — the "split" isn't superstition, it's operations.
3. **(b)** — with term insurance the cheapest product and the best product differ by a pizza per month, and the entire purchase fires exactly once — when your family cannot negotiate. Buy the payer. (Option d — splitting for the sake of it — splits the medical-tests hassle more than it diversifies risk.)
