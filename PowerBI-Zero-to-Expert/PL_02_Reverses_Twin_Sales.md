# 🔙 PL2 · Reverses & Twin Sales: Where Symmetry Lies

> Two watches, sold ₹990 each. One fetched +10%, the other took −10%. Break-even, right? WRONG — you lost ₹20, and you'll lose it every single time, because the losing sale hides a FAT cost price (₹1,100) under the same ₹990 tag. ## 📘 Concepts

### 2.1 The dangerous reverse: division by the 0.8 family

Forward losses shrink (CP 500 − 12% → 440). Reversing them GROWS the number, which feels wrong to freshers and is exactly why exam-setters farm it:

```
Sold Rs 480 at 20% LOSS:   CP = 480 / 0.8 = Rs 600
Sold Rs 1,035 at 10% LOSS: CP = 1,035 / 0.9 = Rs 1,150
```

Losing sales sit on the (1 − rate) multiplier. The reverse always divides by LESS than 1, so CP > SP — if your recovered CP comes out SMALLER than the selling price on a "sold at a loss" question, you've multiplied instead of divided. Sign-check, every time.

### 2.2 The twin-sale theorem (same SP, ±x% — ALWAYS a loss)

```
Watch A: SP 990 at +10%  -> CP = 990/1.1 = 900
Watch B: SP 990 at -10%  -> CP = 990/0.9 = 1,100
Total CP = 2,000 vs Total SP = 1,980  -> LOSS Rs 20 = 1%
LAW: same SP, +x% and -x% -> loss of x^2/100 percent. 10 -> 1%, 20 -> 4%.
```

Why must the loser win? The +10% rides a small CP (900 → tiny +90 gain), the −10% rides a big CP (1,100 → fat −110 hit). Same SP hides unequal bases, and bases are where profit actually lives. Symmetry is a liar; the theorem is not.

### 2.2b The "articles" version (same law, counting costume)

ShopKart clearance scan: two rice-brands tagged ₹990 a bag... same math. But the exam also hides twins as ARTICLES: **CP of 12 pens = SP of 15 pens**:

```
12c = 15s  ->  s/c = 12/15 = 4/5 = 0.8  -> SP is 4/5 of CP -> LOSS 20%
Flip it: CP of 12 = SP of 10 -> s/c = 12/10 = 1.2 -> GAIN 20%
Memory: set the equal-rupees equation, solve s/c, compare with 1.
```

### 2.3 SP-quoted profits (the braggart's base)

"A trader makes 25% of the selling price." Sounds like 25% profit — it isn't, by exam law:

```
SP = 100 -> P = 25 -> CP = 75 -> exam-law P% = 25/75 = 33.33%
Conversion: if profit is (k)% of SP, exam P% = k/(100-k) x 100.
Also common: "profit is x% of CP" is already exam-legal. Only SP-quotes need conversion.
```

ShopKart's 29% MARGIN is precisely this dialect — honest in a P&L, misleading in an exam. Quote the base or be misquoted.

### 2.4 The clean-landing audit

Exam-setters choose CPs that make reverses land round: ₹1,150 ÷ 1.15 = 1,000.00 exactly. If your division yields ₹998.971..., you've (a) divided by the wrong twin (1.15 instead of 0.85-class), or (b) the question is LS (loss) not profit. Lengthy decimals = warning siren, not stubbornness badge. Re-read, re-classify, re-divide.

## ❓ Quiz

**Q1.** Twin watches: both sold ₹990, one at +10%, one at −10%. Overall:
- (a) break-even, obviously
- (b) **LOSS of 1% — CPs 900 + 1,100 = 2,000 vs SP 1,980; same-SP twin sales ALWAYS lose x²/100 = 10²/100 = 1%; the losing sale hides a fat CP under the identical tag**
- (c) gain 1%
- (d) loss 2%

**Q2.** A mixer sold ₹480 at a 20% LOSS. Its CP:
- (a) ₹384
- (b) **₹600 — 480 ÷ 0.8: loss-sales sit on the (1 − rate) multiplier, so the reverse DIVIDES by 0.8 and grows; a CP smaller than SP here would be a multiply-slip confession**
- (c) ₹576
- (d) ₹400

**Q3.** A trader's profit is 25% of the selling price. Exam-law profit% (on CP):
- (a) 25%
- (b) **33.33% — SP 100 → P 25 → CP 75 → 25/75; SP-quoted bragging inflates by exactly the CP:SP gap; k/(100−k) does the conversion in one breath**
- (c) 20%
- (d) 75%

### ✅ Answers

1. **(b)** — the theorem never sleeps: same SP, opposite rates, guaranteed loss x²/100.
2. **(b)** — the 0.8-family divide grows, and any shrinkage is a self-caught error.
3. **(b)** — quote the base or be misquoted; SP-dialect flatters, CP-law settles.
